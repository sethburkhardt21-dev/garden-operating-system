## Site-confirmed facts

- **City:** Richmond, Kentucky
- **County:** Madison County
- **Yard size:** approximately **2 acres**
- **Project type:** residential mixed edible + ornamental garden

Two acres is enough land to avoid forcing incompatible crops into one compact bed. The design should therefore use a **concentrated high-maintenance garden core** near the house/water source, then place lower-frequency perennial, vine, pollinator, and aesthetic features farther out. The entire two acres should **not** automatically become cultivated garden.

## Climate baseline

The 2023 USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map is the authority for perennial cold-hardiness methodology. Richmond/Madison County spans the **6b–7a transition** at city/ZIP scale; the exact parcel can fall on either side depending on location and microclimate.

### Conservative operating rule
Until the parcel is pinned on the USDA map:
- design permanent plants to tolerate **Zone 6b** where practical
- treat Zone 7a-only material as requiring a favorable microclimate or explicit site confirmation
- do not use hardiness zone as a planting-date calendar; frost timing, soil temperature, topography, and current weather control annual crop timing

### Richmond/Madison County implications
- four-season climate
- hot, humid summers
- meaningful fungal-disease pressure on tomatoes, cucurbits, and roses
- spring/fall frost risk varies materially across slopes and low spots
- garlic naturally fits a fall planting cycle
- asparagus and rhubarb require permanent beds
- dahlias require winter-storage planning rather than assuming in-ground survival
- irrigation must handle both humid/wet periods and summer dry spells

## Local research authority

The primary local horticulture resource is the **Madison County Cooperative Extension Service** in Richmond. Local Extension provides horticulture programming and soil-testing support.

Important local advantage: Madison County currently advertises **free soil testing for residents, up to 10 samples per home/farm per calendar year**. A two-acre property should use this to sample biologically different areas separately rather than blending the whole yard into one meaningless average.

## Two-acre design consequences

### What the extra space solves
- room for a true permanent asparagus/rhubarb area
- room for a four-block annual crop rotation if desired
- room to let pumpkins run without swallowing kitchen-garden paths
- room for separate dry-herb and moisture-loving herb zones
- room for roses to have airflow and visual prominence
- room for a real cutting-flower border rather than squeezing flowers into vegetable gaps
- room for a pollinator/native area away from the tightly managed vegetable core
- room for compost/material handling and future rainwater/irrigation infrastructure

### What the extra space does NOT solve
- water access
- sunlight
- drainage
- deer/groundhog pressure
- poor soil
- excessive maintenance burden
- poor path design

A badly spread-out two-acre garden can be harder to maintain than a small garden. Distance from house/water is therefore a design variable.
