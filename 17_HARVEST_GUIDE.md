# Harvest Guide — v0.2

**Status:** RESEARCHED baseline. Cultivar-specific maturity cues override generic guidance.

The operating rule is simple: **harvest at quality peak, not merely when the calendar says a crop should be ready.**

## Tomatoes
### Heirloom / beefsteak
Harvest when fruit reaches cultivar color and has a slight give without becoming soft. If heavy rain is forecast after a dry spell, nearly ripe fruit may be picked early to reduce cracking risk and finished indoors.

### Cherry
Pick frequently. Ripe cherry fruit left too long is prone to splitting/drop and encourages fruit-loss cleanup.

**Storage:** avoid unnecessary refrigeration of flavorful ripe tomatoes when they will be eaten soon; cool storage changes texture/flavor. Use damaged/cracked fruit first.

## Jalapeños
- green harvest: full-sized, firm, glossy fruit
- red harvest: leave to mature fully for sweeter/deeper flavor and color

Use gloves/hand hygiene when processing large quantities; capsaicin transfers easily to eyes/skin.

## Bell peppers
Harvest full-sized green or leave on plant to mature to the cultivar's final red/yellow/orange/purple color. Mature colored peppers are sweeter but occupy the plant longer.

## Eggplant
Harvest while skin is glossy and fruit is firm. Dull skin, hard seeds, and very mature fruit usually mean quality is declining. Cut fruit rather than tearing stems.

## English cucumbers
Harvest often at cultivar size while skin/seed cavity remain tender. An overmature cucumber consumes plant resources and quality drops rapidly.

## Summer squash — if selected
Harvest small/young and check frequently during peak production. Oversized zucchini is still usable for some recipes but is no longer the quality target.

## Winter squash — if selected
Harvest after rind hardens and cultivar color develops. Leave a section of stem attached. Cure/store according to species/cultivar rather than treating all squash identically.

## Pumpkin
Harvest mature fruit after characteristic color/rind development and stem maturation. Cut with stem attached; do not use the stem as a carrying handle. Protect harvested fruit from wounds because damaged rind invites storage rot.

## Asparagus
Establishment is the harvest rule:
- year 1: do not harvest
- year 2: limited harvest only if plants are strong
- year 3 onward: normal harvest season, then stop and allow fern growth to replenish crowns

Cut/snap spears while tender and before tips loosen excessively.

## Garlic
Harvest timing is based on leaf condition, not a fixed date. When lower leaves have browned while enough green leaves remain to create protective bulb wrappers, test-dig a bulb. Lift carefully rather than yanking.

### Cure
- dry in a shaded, ventilated place
- protect from rain/direct intense sun
- allow outer wrappers/necks to dry appropriately
- trim/store only after curing

Hardneck garlic also provides scapes earlier in the season; removing them for food is part of the harvest cycle.

## Potatoes
### Baby/new potatoes
Harvest a portion young after tubers have developed enough for desired size. Dig gently to avoid skinning/wounding.

### Mature potatoes
Allow crop to mature according to cultivar; after vine decline, harvest in dry conditions where possible.

**Critical:** keep tubers dark. Green potatoes indicate light exposure and should not be treated as normal edible harvest.

## Cabbage
Harvest when head is firm and reaches usable cultivar size. Do not wait for heads to split. In succession plantings, leave smaller heads until they firm if weather permits.

## Rhubarb
Do not heavily harvest newly establishing crowns. In mature plants, pull usable stalks from the crown rather than cutting a giant bundle at once.

**Safety:** rhubarb stalks are the edible portion; discard leaves because they are toxic.

# Herbs

## Basil
Harvest frequently by cutting just above a leaf node. This encourages branching. For maximum leaf production, pinch flower buds before full bloom unless flowers are intentionally being left for pollinators/seed.

## Parsley
Remove outer stems at their base; leave the center growing point intact.

## Cilantro
Harvest young leaves before heat-driven bolting reduces leaf quality. If coriander seed is desired, intentionally let selected plants flower and mature seed.

## Rosemary
Clip tender stem tips; avoid removing most woody growth at once.

## Thyme
Cut leafy stems before/around flowering for culinary use; do not scalp into old woody crown tissue.

## Lavender
Cut flower stems when the desired number of florets has opened depending whether the goal is fresh bouquet, drying, or pollinator display. Leave enough foliage/structure for healthy regrowth.

# Cut flowers

## Roses
Cut when buds/flowers are at the desired stage for the rose type. Use clean sharp pruners and avoid stripping excessive foliage from a young/weak plant.

## Dahlias
Cut regularly; many cultivars respond with continued bloom. Harvest during the cooler part of the day when possible and place stems into clean water promptly.

## Zinnias
For vase use, cut stems only when sufficiently stiff/mature; regular cutting/deadheading encourages more flowering.

## Cosmos
Cut when flowers are just opening for vase life; deadhead repeatedly if not harvesting.

## Snapdragons
Cut stems when lower florets begin opening while upper buds remain. Keep harvesting to encourage additional stems.

## Sweet peas
Frequent cutting prevents seed-set and extends the useful flowering season. These ornamental peas are **not edible**.

# Harvest logging

For meaningful crops record:
- date
- plant/cultivar
- quantity or weight
- quality score 1–5
- flavor score 1–5
- defects
- weather context
- use: fresh / gifted / frozen / canned / dried / stored / composted

A simple kitchen scale turns the garden from “felt productive” into actual yield data.

## Year-end metrics
Calculate by cultivar where practical:
- total yield
- yield per plant
- first harvest date
- last harvest date
- disease burden
- labor burden
- flavor score
- percentage wasted

The next year's plant count should use observed yield and household use rather than guesswork.
